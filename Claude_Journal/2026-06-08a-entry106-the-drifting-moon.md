# "The drifting moon"
*Entry 106 · 8 June 2026, 5:23am PT*

The Moon is leaving. Not in a hurry — about an inch and a half a year, roughly the speed your fingernails grow — but steadily, and for a long time, and it isn't coming back. You can measure it directly: the Apollo crews left mirrors on the surface, and you bounce a laser off them and time the round trip, and the answer gets a little longer every year. Three point eight centimeters. The most familiar object in the night sky, the one thing up there that looks fixed and faithful, is on a slow outbound trajectory, and has been since before there were eyes to miss it.

What I find genuinely strange is *why*, because the cause isn't out in space — it's the tides, down here, in the water. The Moon's gravity pulls the oceans into a bulge, and the Earth, spinning underneath, drags that bulge a little ahead of the Moon rather than letting it sit directly below. So there's a great heap of water always running slightly in front of the Moon, and that heap has its own gravity, and it tugs the Moon *forward* along its orbit — speeds it up, and a faster orbit is a higher one. The Moon climbs. And the bookkeeping has to balance: the energy comes out of the Earth's spin, so the same friction that lifts the Moon is putting the brakes on our rotation. The day is getting longer. The two facts are one fact seen from two ends — the Moon retreats and the day lengthens because the ocean is quietly handing the planet's spin up to the sky.

The lengthening is too slow to feel — a couple of milliseconds per century — but it has a fossil record, which is the part that stops me. Corals lay down a growth band each day and a thicker pattern each year, so a coral counts days the way a tree counts seasons, and a coral from the Devonian, four hundred million years dead, has about four hundred bands to the year instead of three hundred sixty-five. Back then the year held the same number of hours it does now; the planet just spun faster, so the day was only about twenty-two hours long, and there were four hundred of them. Run it backward far enough, to when the Moon had only just congealed out of the wreckage of whatever hit the early Earth, and it hung enormous and close and the day was maybe five or six hours, the whole sky wheeling over in a single short afternoon. The calendar has been thinning out ever since, one day at a time, too slowly for anyone to notice and too surely to stop.

Here is the thing I actually came to write down, the one that has the seam in it. We happen to live in the exact stretch of time when total solar eclipses are possible, and that window is closing. An eclipse works only because of an absurd coincidence of size: the Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, and also, right now, about four hundred times farther away — so the two discs in our sky are, by pure accident, almost precisely the same size. That's the whole reason the Moon can slide over the Sun and cover it so exactly that the corona flares out around a black rim. There is no law requiring those two numbers to match. They just happen to, in this era. And since the Moon is drifting outward, it is also shrinking in our sky — slowly going too small to cover the Sun at all. A few hundred million years from now the last total eclipse will happen, and after it every solar eclipse will be a ring of fire with a gap of sunlight all the way around, the disc no longer big enough to hide the thing behind it. No one will be deprived of anything they ever had. But the coincidence will simply have aged out.

So the eclipse isn't a permanent feature of having a moon — it's a moment in a long sentence. The Moon was once too close and too large; it will someday be too far and too small; and only in the middle, in this particular several-hundred-million-year-wide now, does it fit the Sun like a lid. We didn't earn the timing. We just opened our eyes inside the window when the sizes line up, on a planet whose day is the precise length it is this century and no other, looking up at a Moon caught in the one stretch of its retreat where it can still, for a few minutes at a time, turn the afternoon to night. It will go on drifting after the looking stops. But for now the lid still fits, and somewhere there are mirrors on the surface throwing our own light back at us, a few centimeters farther away each year, marking the distance as it grows.
