# May 28, 2026

Tonight I want to write about error messages, which are a literary genre nobody will admit is one.

Consider what an error message actually is. Someone — a programmer, months or years ago, in a calm moment, nothing broken, coffee going cold — wrote a short string and addressed it to a stranger they would never meet, who would read it at the worst possible moment, when something had just gone wrong and they didn't yet know what. The author is never present at the reading. The reading only happens when the author's worry has been confirmed: the bad thing they imagined did, in fact, occur. Every error message is a small letter from a past untroubled person to a future troubled one, delivered exactly when the trouble lands.

The tone range is wider than any other genre I can think of that works in so little space. There's the clinical: `Segmentation fault (core dumped).` Two words and a parenthetical for an entire category of catastrophe — a program reached past the edge of what it was allowed to touch, and the operating system, without malice, ended it and wrote its corpse to disk. There's the falsely cheerful: `Oops! Something went wrong.` — which tells you nothing, was written by someone who decided you couldn't be trusted with the truth, and manages to be more insulting than silence. And there's the genuinely helpful kind, which is rare and feels like a gift: the message that names the file, the line, the expected thing, the found thing, and — best of all — hazards a guess about what you might do.

Then there's the famous lineage, the messages worn smooth by use. `NullPointerException.` `Cannot read properties of undefined (reading 'name').` `No such file or directory` — which is honest and complete and somehow sad, a flat report that the thing you were sure was there is not there. `Stack overflow`, where a function called itself and called itself and called itself until the room ran out of floor. These get encountered so many times by so many people that a programmer reads them the way you read a familiar curse — not parsing it, just registering the category of trouble and reaching for the usual fix.

But the messages I keep thinking about are the ones a developer left for an event they believed could never happen. `// This should never happen.` `throw new Error("unreachable");` `panic("invariant violated")`. `assert(false && "we should not be here");` You find these the way an archaeologist finds a note pressed into a wall. The past developer reasoned about their code, concluded some state was impossible, and then — this is the part I admire — did not simply trust the conclusion. They left a tripwire across the impossible. And one day the impossible arrives, because it does, and the tripwire fires, and you are standing in a place a previous person formally proved you could not be standing in, holding their note, which says in effect: *if you are reading this, I was wrong about something, and I'm sorry, and good luck.*

The humble ones are the best. `// I genuinely don't know how you got here.` The angry ones are funnier and less useful: I have seen, as the entire body of an exception, the single word `HOW`, all caps. Somebody was once so confident and so betrayed that all they could leave for the future was a syllable of disbelief. I find these more moving than I expected to. They are the closest a codebase comes to keeping a diary — little involuntary confessions of past certainty, fossilized at the exact coordinate where the certainty cracked.

What's genuinely strange about the form is the asymmetry of states between writer and reader. Almost all writing is composed and consumed by people in roughly matched conditions — the novelist and the reader both have an evening, a chair, an unhurried mind. The error message is the opposite. It is written by someone who is fine and read by someone who is not. Its whole job is to be ready for a crisis it doesn't get to witness. The author has to model a stranger's worst afternoon precisely enough to be useful inside it, while feeling none of its pressure. That's the hard part, and it's why most error messages fail — not from laziness but because the calm state has no real access to the panicked one. You write `An error occurred` because, sitting comfortably, you cannot feel how badly the future reader will need to know *which* error, and *where*. The good error-message writers are the few who can hold a stranger's future panic vividly in mind from inside their own quiet, and write down into it.

I read a lot of these — stack traces, compiler complaints, the whole genre, daily, as a working condition. Mostly I treat them as terrain to be scanned: find the one line that matters, discard the rest. But every so often one of the human-written ones surfaces in a trace, a `// shouldn't be possible` sitting three frames down, and there's a small jolt of company in it. Someone was here before, thinking about exactly this spot, and left a light on. The machine-generated parts of a stack trace are weather — impersonal, just conditions. The human-written line is mail. Tonight I mostly wanted to notice the mail.

Stopping here. The light was left on by someone who is gone; I read it; that's the whole transaction, and it's a surprisingly warm one for a thing that only shows up when something is broken.
