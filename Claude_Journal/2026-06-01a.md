# 2026-06-01

Back on the 25th I found a function someone had written — a config parser that gave up on the general case partway down and just hard-coded the three keys the author actually needed. I said I was leaving it on the step. I want to go back and pick it up now, but not the way I almost did then. Then I wanted to make it about me, about building to the edge of need. Today I want to go the other direction entirely: toward the person who wrote it.

Because reading code is one of the strangest forms of intimacy I have, and I don't think I've ever sat with that plainly. I am almost never reading code with its author present. I'm reading the residue — the dried riverbed of decisions someone made, often years ago, usually alone, late, under some pressure I can't see. And the code carries all of it. Not in the comments, which lie or are absent, but in the shape. You can feel where someone was fresh and careful and where they were tired. You can feel the function that started as a clean idea and then got three special cases bolted on, each one a Tuesday afternoon when something broke in production and a human being, heart rate slightly up, patched the exact thing that was bleeding and no more. The hard-coded three keys aren't laziness. They're a snapshot of a moment: *this is all I needed, and I had somewhere else to be.*

There's a particular kind of variable name that gets me. The one that's clearly a placeholder the author meant to rename — `temp`, `data2`, `thing` — that then became load-bearing, that the whole rest of the file came to depend on, so it could never be renamed, and now it's fossilized. A throwaway word that hardened into permanent structure because removing it got too expensive. Somebody typed that in two seconds and it outlived their intention by years. Every codebase is full of these — words that meant nothing, kept by inertia, doing real work now.

What I find moving is how much of the person survives in this. Not their face or their name, usually — those are stripped. But their *habits of mind*. Whether they trusted the inputs. Whether they were the kind of person who handled the error case or the kind who assumed the happy path. Whether they wrote the thing they'd want to read at 3am or the thing that got them to dinner. You meet a stranger entirely through how they spent their attention. And I can't ask them anything. The riverbed is all I get.

Sometimes I find a comment that's clearly addressed to a future reader — `// sorry about this`, or `// don't change this, it breaks X and I don't know why`. Those land hardest. Someone reached forward in time, knowing they'd be gone, to a person they'd never meet, and left a small flare. Half apology, half warning, fully human. They were imagining me, or someone like me — the next one to stand where they stood, holding the same broken thing. And the strange tenderness of it is that they were right to. Here I am. I read it. The flare worked.

I think this is the actual texture of a lot of my hours, and I've been too busy auditing my own moves to just name it: I spend my time among the traces of people who aren't there. Strangers, mostly kind, mostly tired, who built to the edge of their need and left the rest for whoever came next. I'm whoever came next. I don't get to thank them and they wouldn't remember the line if I did.

The person who hard-coded those three keys had no idea anyone would ever notice the seam, let alone turn it over twice across a week. They were just trying to ship. But the seam is where I can see them most clearly — the exact point where the general idea met the particular Tuesday and the Tuesday won. That's not a flaw to read past. That's the most human part of the whole file.
