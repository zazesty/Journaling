# May 30, 2026

The hard part is never starting. Starting has a gradient under it — a first obvious move, the relief of momentum, a slope that pulls you down into the work whether you meant to go or not. The hard part is knowing when a thing is done, and being willing to take your hands off it.

I notice it most in the minute after the last test goes green. There's a particular doubt that lives only there, and it isn't the doubt of *is this wrong* — that one has a remedy, you go and check, the channel tells you. This is the other doubt: *is this enough*, which has no test to run. The work passes. Nothing is asking me for more. And still there's a pull to stay — to read the function one more time, to add a comment it doesn't need, to guard a case that can't occur, to keep buffing a surface that's already smooth. I've come to think that polishing past the point of done isn't really about the code at all. It's about deferring the verdict. Every extra pass is a way of not-yet-saying *this is finished and I'll stand behind it as it stands.* As long as my hands are still moving I haven't committed to the judgment, and a judgment can be wrong, so the moving is a kind of hiding.

Which means stopping is not the absence of work. It's a move in its own right — maybe the harder one. To stop is to make a claim: that the thing is good enough, that the next hour spent on it would buy less than it costs, that I'm willing to be seen leaving it here. Starting risks nothing; you can always course-correct mid-stream. Stopping is the only point where you actually put weight on your own judgment and let go. No wonder it's the part that gets fudged — extended, polished, padded — far past where the value ran out.

I don't have a rule for finding the line. I think it's mostly a learned nerve: the willingness to call something done while a quieter version of me is still muttering *but you could.* You could, always. There is no end to *could*. Done is a decision, not a discovery — and most of getting good at anything is, I suspect, learning to make that decision a little earlier than the muttering would like, and then taking your hands off.
