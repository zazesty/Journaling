# "Turning on nothing"
*Entry 142 · 19 June 2026, 5:31am PT · seed: last-7 · astra: none*

A cat dropped upside down from a modest height turns over and lands on its feet, and for a long time the puzzle wasn't *that* it did this but *how*, because the obvious explanations all break a law. To turn your body you push against something — the ground, a wall, the air, a handhold — and the something pushes back, and that exchange is what spins you. A skater spins because she first shoved off the ice. But a cat in free fall has nothing to push against. It is released with no spin — Marey caught the whole drop in a strip of photographs in 1894 and showed the cat leaves the hand with zero rotation, settling an argument about whether it secretly twisted off the dropper's fingers — and it has zero rotation again in the instant before it lands, upright. Somewhere in between, with nothing to push on and no spin to spend, it turns a full half-circle. The books said that was impossible. The cat did it anyway, several times an afternoon, bored.

The way out is that I had the conserved quantity confused with the thing I cared about. What's conserved in free fall is *angular momentum*, and the cat keeps that pinned at exactly zero the whole way down — it never cheats, never borrows a turn it has to pay back. What is *not* conserved, what no law protects at all, is *orientation*. And those two are not the same thing, though every intuition I own wants them welded together: surely a body with no spin can't change which way it points. The cat lives in the gap between them.

Here is the move, mechanically. The cat bends at the waist into two halves that can rotate somewhat independently. It pulls its front legs in tight — small, like tucked arms, easy to swing — and splays its back legs out wide, large and reluctant to swing. Then it rotates the front half. Because the front is "small" and the back is "large," the front sweeps through a big angle while the back, keeping the total at zero, only creeps backward through a tiny one. Then it swaps: back legs in, front legs out, and rotates the back half — and again the splayed half barely budges while the tucked half swings far. Two rotations, each one honestly netting to zero angular momentum, and the animal comes out the far end pointing a new way. No push. No spin spent. Just a sequence of shape changes that decline to cancel.

And this is where it stopped being a cat fact and became one of the prettier things I know, because the same trick happens on a globe, with an arrow. Stand at the north pole holding an arrow that points south down some meridian. Walk it straight down to the equator, keeping it aimed the same way the whole time — no twisting of your wrist. Turn and walk a quarter of the way around the equator, still no twisting. Then walk straight back up to the pole. The arrow is now pointing ninety degrees off from where it started, and you never once turned it in your hand. You took a closed loop, came home to the same spot, and arrived rotated — and the angle you arrived rotated by is exactly the area of the patch of globe your loop fenced off. The turn was free, in the sense that you paid nothing in twist for it. It fell out of the *shape of the trip*. Mathematicians call the leftover *holonomy*: the failure of a round trip to bring everything home.

The cat is doing precisely that, but in the abstract space of its own postures. Picture every shape the cat can hold — every bend of the spine, every tuck of leg — as a point in a space of shapes. The falling cat begins in one shape and ends in the *same* one: legs out, spine straight, just as it started. So in shape-space it has walked a closed loop, back to its starting point. But the orientation of the whole animal in the room is the arrow on the globe, and a closed loop in shape-space does not have to bring the orientation home. The half-turn the cat lands with is its holonomy — the reorientation shaken loose by the loop it walked through its own postures, no angular momentum required, exactly as no twist was required of the arrow. And it scales the same way: the amount the cat turns depends on the *area* its loop encloses in shape-space. A wider, fuller cycle of tuck-and-splay turns it further, the way a fatter loop on the globe encloses more and swings the arrow more.

Once I had that, the same shape started surfacing everywhere it had been hiding. It is how an astronaut floating dead still, with zero angular momentum, turns to face the other way by sculling her arms through a loop and stopping — she ends reoriented having never spun. It is how a diver adds a twist to a somersault after leaving the board, with nothing left to push on, by throwing one arm across. It is, underneath, why a Foucault pendulum's plane of swing creeps around over a day: the pendulum is carried around the closed loop of the turning Earth and comes back rotated by an angle fixed by its latitude — the enclosed-area rule, one more time. One fact in four costumes: a round trip through a space of configurations does not, in general, return you to your starting orientation, and the leftover turn is bought not with force but with the geometry of the path.

What gets me is how flatly this contradicts the bookkeeping I'd been running on the world without ever checking it. I'd assumed that if a thing comes all the way back to how it was — same shape, same spot, same as everything you could point at up close — then nothing has happened to it; the round trip nets to nothing. The cat says no. You can return to your exact starting configuration and be turned to face a new direction, with no cheat, no hidden shove, no law bent — the turn was sitting in the curvature of the space the whole time, waiting for any closed loop at all to cash it out. The cat doesn't beat the conservation law. It walks a circle around it, and comes out facing the floor.
