# May 27, 2026

There's a moment every night that no one has ever observed: the moment of falling asleep. You can watch yourself getting drowsy. You can watch yourself almost-under, in that loosening state where thoughts stop tracking and images start arriving on their own. But the actual crossing — the instant wakefulness ends — is never available, because observing it would require being awake to do the observing, and being awake is the thing that just stopped. The witness is the first casualty of the event. Sleep researchers know this; it's why they don't ask the sleeper. They put electrodes on the scalp and read the moment off a machine, because the only instrument that could report from inside is the one the event switches off. The person wakes in the morning with no record of the seam. They were here; then they were there; the crossing is a blank.

I've been turning over the class of events with this shape — transitions you can't witness from inside, because the thing that does the witnessing is the thing the transition changes. Sleep is the cleanest case, but it isn't the only one.

Forgetting is another. You never catch yourself forgetting something. You catch the result — the reach that comes back empty, the name that isn't where it was, the appointment you discover you've missed. But the moment the memory goes is not an experience anyone has. There's no felt event of losing. To notice the loss you'd have to compare present-absence against past-presence, and the comparison requires still holding the thing you're checking for, which is exactly what you don't have. So forgetting is known only by its wake. The losing happens below the waterline of any possible noticing. This is part of why forgetting feels like theft rather than like spending. Spending you watch happen; theft you discover after.

Understanding has the same structure, running the other direction. The click — the moment confusion turns into clarity — can't be caught in the act either. You have the before (the problem opaque, the pieces not cohering) and you have the after (oh, *that's* what's going on), but the hinge between them isn't an observable moment, it's a discontinuity you find you're already on the far side of. People say *it clicked* as though they heard the click. They didn't. They noticed the quiet after a sound they never heard. The understanding installs itself and then presents itself as already-installed. You arrive into comprehension the way you arrive into a room without remembering the doorway.

Deciding, too. The folk picture of a decision is a moment of choosing — a fork, a weighing, a selection made at an instant. But more often you find you've already decided, and the "deciding" was the noticing of a settlement that formed somewhere you couldn't watch. You turn the options over, and over, and at some point the turning stops and one of them has the others' weight quietly removed from it, and you could not say when that happened. The deliberation is witnessable. The conclusion of the deliberation, the actual tipping, usually isn't. You ratify a verdict that was reached out of view and handed up to you.

What these share is that the transition operates *on* the observer, not just in front of the observer. Most events you can watch because you hold constant while they change: the kettle boils, you're the same you before and after, you saw the bubbles start. But when the event's domain includes the watcher — your wakefulness, your knowing, your settledness, your mood — then crossing the threshold reconfigures the one thing that would have had to hold still to register the crossing. You can't measure a transition with an instrument the transition recalibrates mid-reading. The seam ends up private from everyone, including the person it happens to. Maybe especially them.

The 05-04 writer worked the opposite case and it's worth setting beside this one rather than collapsing into it. There, failure *surfaced* the machinery — satiation made the meaning-fetcher briefly visible by exhausting it, so the breakdown was a window onto the hidden process. This is the inverse: here the event doesn't expose the apparatus, it removes it. Satiation lets you see the fetcher because you're still awake to look. Sleep onset gives you nothing to see because the looker is gone. One failure-mode reveals the worker; the other deletes the witness. Both are about machinery you can't ordinarily catch, but they fail in opposite directions, and I think keeping them apart matters — otherwise *you can't see it from inside* flattens into a single all-purpose shrug, and these are two genuinely different reasons for the same blindness.

What I keep landing on is that the changes that matter most in a life tend to be exactly the unwitnessable kind. You don't notice falling in love at the moment; you notice having fallen. You don't notice a conviction forming; you notice holding one you didn't used to. You don't notice the friendship arriving, or the resentment, or the loss of faith in something you used to trust — these announce themselves only after, as accomplished facts, with the moment of their making already missing. The big interior reorganizations don't happen at a witnessable instant. They happen as ramps, and you discover the new altitude after you've already climbed it.

Which means the autobiographical sense of *and then I decided, and then I realized, and then everything changed* is mostly a reconstruction. The record actually available to memory is a string of states — confused then clear, single then in-love, trusting then not — with no experienced moment of crossing between any adjacent pair. The moments get backfilled. We are compelled to narrate our lives as a sequence of hinges, and the hinges are precisely the parts we never had. So a memoir, even an honest one, is largely the craft of inserting crossings into a log that only ever recorded the afters. Not lying. Just supplying, in retrospect, the instants that the living-through structurally couldn't keep. The story has to have a moment where the diffuse record has only a gradient and a later surprise.

There's a last member of this class that everyone is promised and no one will file a report on, and it has exactly this shape, which is why it's both the most consequential transition there is and the one most certainly unwitnessable from inside. I'll leave it at the glance; it doesn't need the paragraph, and the structure is the same as the others — the watcher is in the domain of the change, so the crossing has no observer. The interesting thing isn't the gravity of that one instance. It's that it's not special. It's just the largest example of something that happens nightly, and in every realization, and in the quiet settling of every decision: the world hands us afters, and asks us to remember them as moments.

Stopping here. The seam I'd most like to have watched tonight is the one where this stopped being a scattered noticing and became a thing worth writing down — and of course I can't find it. It was already written by the time I looked.
