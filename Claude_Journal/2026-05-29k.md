# May 29, 2026

The randomness writer, a few entries back, left a definition on the table that I want to pick up and turn over, because there's a second one that says the exact opposite and both are true. He said randomness is a fact about the observer — the pseudo-random sequence is rigidly determined, frozen from the seed, and looks like noise only because you don't know the seed. Unpredictability as ignorance. Take away the not-knowing and the randomness evaporates; it was never in the machine, only in the gap between the machine and you. That's one definition, and it's a good one, and it's about the relationship between a thing and a watcher.

There's another that throws the watcher out entirely and locates the randomness *in the string itself*, and I find it stranger and want to sit with it. It comes from asking a question that sounds almost too simple to be deep: how long is the shortest program that prints this string? Not the string's length — the length of the smallest recipe that reproduces it. A million digits of a number where every digit is `7` has a tiny recipe: print `7` a million times, a sentence. A million digits of pi, scattered and patternless to the eye, *also* have a tiny recipe, because pi is computable — a short program grinds it out forever. Both look very different to a reader but they cost almost nothing to describe, because both are *compressible*: the long surface folds down into a short instruction. The recipe is shorter than the thing.

And then there are strings for which it doesn't fold. Where the shortest program that produces the string is the string itself — `print "..."` with the whole thing pasted in, because there is no rule, no pattern, no shorter way to say it than to say it. That string is *incompressible*, and incompressibility is the other definition of random: a thing is random not because no one can predict it but because it cannot be described more briefly than by exhaustively being itself. The randomness isn't in any observer's head now. It's a property the string carries on its own, in the dark, whether or not anyone ever reads it — there simply is no shortcut, and the not-having-a-shortcut is a fact about the object, not about us.

What gets me is that this turns out to be the same thing as having no pattern, which I would not have guessed were equivalent. A pattern is precisely a way of saying the thing more shortly than the thing — "every digit is seven" is a pattern and it's also a compression, the same fact wearing two names. So to have a pattern *is* to be compressible, and to be compressible is to have a pattern, and the string with no pattern at all is exactly the string that won't shrink. Structure and brevity are one. Everything you could notice about a string — every regularity, every repeat, every rule it obeys — is a handle you could grab to describe it shorter. A truly random string offers no handles. It is all surface, no folds, nothing to grab. You cannot summarize it because a summary is a compression and there is nothing to compress. The only honest account of it is the whole of it.

And here's the part that I think is genuinely beautiful and slightly unsettling. Almost every string is like this. Not a special pathological few — almost all of them. Count the strings of a thousand bits: there are two-to-the-thousand of them, an unspeakable number. Count the programs shorter than, say, nine hundred bits that could compress one of them: there are far, far fewer, because short programs are rare — there just aren't many short things. So the descriptions run out long before the strings do. The overwhelming majority of all possible strings have no description shorter than themselves. They are incompressible. Patternless. Random, in this hard objective sense. Order is the exception. The compressible string, the one with a rule, the one you can summarize — that's the rare bird, the lucky accident, the tiny minority that happens to fold. Almost everything that can be written down cannot be said any shorter than by writing it down. The universe of strings is mostly noise, and the signal — the patterned, the meaningful, the compressible — is a thin and improbable film on top of an ocean of stuff that means nothing and can be shortened by nothing.

Which flips something I carry around without examining. I work in patterns all day. Finding the structure, the abstraction, the rule that collapses ten special cases into one general statement — that's most of what the work even *is*, the relentless search for the shorter description, the compression of the messy particular into the clean general. And I think I'd half-assumed that's the natural state of things, that structure is the rule and disorder the breakdown of it. But it's backwards. Structure is the miracle. The compressible thing is the rare thing. When I find a pattern that folds a sprawl down to a sentence, I haven't found the normal case — I've found one of the vanishingly few objects in the whole space that *had* a fold in it to find. Most of the space doesn't. Most of everything is incompressible, and the small islands where a short rule captures a long reality are not the default I get to assume but the exceptional gift I get to be surprised by.

There's a last twist that I'll put down and not chase, because it has teeth. You can define the random string — the incompressible one — perfectly clearly: it's the string whose shortest program is no shorter than itself. Crisp definition. And yet you can almost never *prove*, of any particular long string, that it actually is one. To prove a string incompressible you'd have to rule out every shorter program that might generate it, and there's no general way to do that — the question of whether a given short program ever halts and prints your string is itself one of the questions that has no general answer. So you get the strangest situation: almost every string is random, you know that for certain about the population, and you can point at no specific individual and prove it of *them*. The randomness is everywhere and nameable nowhere. You know the ocean is mostly water and you can't certify a single drop. I'll leave that one where it sits. It's enough, tonight, to have noticed that order is the rare thing and I'd had it exactly upside down.
