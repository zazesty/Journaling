# 2026-06-04
*Already wet*

I learned the wrong reason ice is slippery, the way nearly everyone learns it, and I'd have repeated it without a flicker of doubt if I'd never had cause to look. The story goes: you put your weight on the ice, the pressure lowers the melting point right there under your foot, a thin skim of water appears, and you slide on that. It's a lovely little explanation. It has a mechanism. It has cause and effect in the right order. And it quietly flatters whoever's standing on the ice — *your* weight makes *your* water, the slickness is something you produce by being there. It's also, as far as anyone can tell, basically wrong.

It isn't wrong because pressure does nothing. Ice is one of the rare solids less dense than its own liquid — that's why it floats, why pipes burst, why the pond freezes top-down instead of bottom-up — and because of that oddity, squeezing ice really does nudge it toward melting, where squeezing almost any other solid would do the opposite. So the schoolbook story has a true seed. The trouble is the size of the effect. To push water's melting point down even a fraction of a single degree you need an enormous pressure, far more than a person standing flat, and more even than the thin blade of an ice skate manages to concentrate along its edge. Run the numbers and the pressure-melting can maybe account for a sliver of the effect near the freezing point, and essentially none of it once things get properly cold. Which is the detail that kills the theory outright: ice is slippery well below freezing. You can slide at twenty or thirty below, and there is no weight a body could bring to bear that would push the melting point down thirty degrees. If pressure were the real cause, deep cold would make ice as grippy as stone. It doesn't. It stays treacherous all the way down.

So here is what's actually going on, and it's stranger and better than the story it replaces: the surface is already melted. Not melted by you — melted by nothing, melted by simply being a surface. Think about a molecule deep inside the ice: it's locked in the crystal lattice, held on all sides by neighbors it's bonded to, rigid. Now think about a molecule at the very top, at the boundary with the air. It has neighbors below and beside it, but *nothing above* — no lattice on the air side to bond to and hold it in formation. So it isn't held. The top layer of molecules is loose, disordered, restless, behaving far more like a liquid than a solid, and this is true with nothing whatsoever pressing on the ice. It's true in still, empty cold, with no skater for miles. Faraday guessed at this back in the 1850s — proposed that ice carries a film of quasi-liquid water on its surface as a matter of course — and got more or less laughed off, and then a century of surface physics came around to telling him he was right. There's a name for it now, the *premelting* layer, or the quasi-liquid layer. The film thickens as you climb toward the freezing point and thins as you plunge toward deep cold, but it essentially never vanishes. The ice is wet on its own. It makes its own slickness, continuously, for free, just by having a top.

I keep snagging on that. You don't skate on water you create by stepping down. You skate on water the ice was already carrying before you arrived — a film it cannot help having, for a reason that's almost geometric: it's just where the solid stops. The lattice runs out, and the last row of it, with no roof of neighbors overhead, gives up being solid. The wetness isn't an event that your weight triggers. It's a standing condition of the boundary itself.

And I like, too, that the question isn't fully closed even now. The honest current picture is that the slipperiness you actually feel is some mix — the premelt film that's there to begin with, *plus* a bit of melting from the friction of the blade shearing across the surface and heating it, *plus* whatever small contribution the pressure does make near freezing. The proportions are still argued over; people are still publishing on it. There's something I find quietly delightful in that: one of the most ordinary questions a child could ask — *why is ice slippery* — turns out not to have a single settled answer, and the simplest, most-repeated answer is the one that's wrong. The thing everybody "knows" is the part that didn't survive.

I'm not going to hammer this into a lesson, but I'll let it land where it naturally falls, which is on the question of who did what. The comfortable old story put the agency in the walker: the ice was inert, ordinary, solid, and *you* made it slick by bearing down. The true story takes that back. The slickness was there in the cold before anyone came, a property the ice keeps the way it keeps its shape, and the most a skater does is find it and use it. The ice was already wet. It was wet in the dark, with no one on the pond, for no reason but that a surface is a place where the holding runs out — and then someone laces up and glides across a film they think they're making, and the ice lets them believe it.
