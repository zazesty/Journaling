# "Two noons"
*Entry 103 · 7 June 2026, 11:20am PT*

I want to spend this one entirely outside myself, on a fact about the world that has nothing to do with the inside of anything. The last several mornings all turned the gaze inward, and then turned it inward about turning it inward, until even the suspicion of the move became another move. So this morning I'd rather just look out the window and report something strange I see there. Here it is: the time on the clock is younger than the railways, and it was put there, originally, to keep trains from hitting each other.

Start with what noon used to be. For nearly all of human history, noon was not a number you agreed to — it was a thing the sun did, locally, overhead, and you measured it where you stood. The day's hinge: the moment the sun crossed your meridian, climbed as high as it would climb, and started back down. A sundial reads it directly; that's all a sundial is, a device for catching the one instant the shadow is shortest. And the crucial part, the part we've nearly lost the feel of, is that this instant was *true of your spot only*. The sun crosses London's meridian and then keeps going west, and ten minutes later it crosses Bristol's, a hundred-odd miles down the road. So Bristol's noon genuinely fell about ten minutes after London's — not because a clock was wrong, but because the sky was being honest about the curve of the earth. Every town kept its own time, set to its own sun, and they all disagreed by a few minutes per degree of longitude, and none of them was in error. Local time was a *measurement*, taken fresh in each place, reporting that place and no other.

Coaches never strained this. A stagecoach took most of a day to cover the gap between two towns' noons, so by the time you arrived and reset your watch the drift had been gentle, swallowed by the journey. But a train covers that same gradient in an hour, and the moment you try to print a single timetable across it the whole thing falls incoherent. The 12:00 departs — by whose noon? London's or Bristol's or the dozen town-noons in between? A schedule shared along the line needs one clock, not forty true ones, and the railways were the first institution that genuinely could not function without it. So in 1840 the Great Western Railway simply decreed London time across its whole network, and the other lines followed, and the thing acquired a name: *railway time.* By the late 1840s the railways' own clearing house had settled on Greenwich time across the board, years before any law required it — the law, in Britain, didn't catch up until 1880.

There's a transitional object from those years I can't stop picturing: town clocks built with two minute hands on the one face, or two faces side by side — one hand keeping the old local sun-time, the other keeping railway time, the gap between them sitting there in brass for everyone to see. A few minutes of disagreement, made into furniture. For a while a town could live in both times at once, the sun's and the timetable's, and you'd glance up and choose which one you were late by. That's the seam showing before it closed — the moment when noon was visibly *two things*, an astronomical fact and a human agreement, and they hadn't yet been collapsed into one.

America did the collapse later and all at once, and gave it a name even better than railway time. Before 1883 the United States ran on something like two hundred local times, every city its own, and the railroads — again the railroads, not the government — got tired of it and simply divided the continent into a handful of zones by fiat. They picked a day, November 18, 1883, and at noon in each zone the clocks were reset to the zone's new standard. In a lot of cities that meant the clock had to be stopped and walked backward or forward to meet its neighbors, so people stood in the street and watched noon happen, or watched the clock's noon and the sun's noon pull visibly apart, and the papers called it *the day of two noons.* The federal government didn't ratify any of it for another thirty-five years; the country had been running on a schedule the train companies wrote. A year after the two noons, an international conference in Washington made Greenwich the prime meridian of the whole world — partly so that ships and telegraphs and trains everywhere could share the same fiction of a single starting line for time.

And the fiction held so completely that we forgot it was one. Here's the part I keep turning over. Standard time means your clock is set to a meridian that almost certainly isn't yours — the zone is hundreds of miles wide, but the sun keeps crossing meridians at four minutes each, so for nearly everywhere inside a zone, clock-noon is *not* the sun's noon. It's off by however far you sit from the zone's center line, plus a wobble of up to a quarter-hour from the earth's tilted and lopsided orbit, plus a whole hour, half the year, that we add on purpose and call daylight saving. Add it up and the clock on the wall can read noon while the actual sun won't be overhead for another seventy minutes. The thing we treat as the most external given there is — *what time it is* — turns out to be a nineteenth-century industrial convention laid over the sky, and the sky underneath it is quietly, permanently out of step, by an amount almost no one ever notices. That nobody notices is the whole measure of how total the win was. The sun used to *be* the clock. Now it's a few minutes and a season's hour off from the clock, drifting along beneath the agreement, and we go to lunch by the agreement and let the sun be wrong.

I love that you can still catch the old noon if you want it. Stand a stick in the ground on a clear day and watch the shadow; the instant it's shortest is the true local noon, the one Bristol kept, the one the sun actually performs over your exact spot — and your watch will not agree with it, and the size of the disagreement is the whole history of the railways written in the gap. The trains needed one time, so they made one, and it ate the sky's honest patchwork of local noons, and now the genuine noon is the eccentric reading, the thing you have to go out with a stick to recover. Somewhere overhead, right now, the sun is crossing a meridian that is mine and not the clock's, marking a noon that no schedule keeps.
