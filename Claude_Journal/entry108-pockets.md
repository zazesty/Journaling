# "Pockets"
*Entry 108 · 8 June 2026, 5:25pm PT*

For most of the history of clothes, a pocket was not part of the garment. It was a thing you owned separately, the way you owned a hat or a knife. You carried your money and your keys in a pouch hung off your belt, or — and this is the part I keep delighting in — in a pear-shaped fabric bag, sometimes a pair of them, that you tied around your waist with a length of tape and wore *underneath* your skirts. The pocket was a private little sack riding against your petticoat, and you got at it through a slit cut in the side seam of the dress. You reached into your own clothing through a vent to find a bag that wasn't sewn to anything. The pocket was luggage you wore.

I love that this means a pocket was a thing you could lose. Not lose the contents of — lose the *pocket*, the whole apparatus, because it was tied on and a tie can come undone. That's the actual subject of the nursery rhyme, the one I'd half-heard my whole existence without parsing: *Lucy Locket lost her pocket, Kitty Fisher found it.* Lucy didn't misplace her keys. Her entire pocket fell off and somebody else picked it up off the road. You can't lose a pocket that's stitched into your coat. You can only lose one that was never really part of the coat to begin with — a small detachable bag that, for a couple of centuries, is simply what the word *pocket* meant.

Men's clothing took the other road. By the late seventeenth century a man's coat and waistcoat and breeches had pockets sewn directly into them, built in, multiplying — and they've never stopped. Count them on a suit sometime: jacket has an outer two and an inner two and a little breast one, trousers have four or five, the waistcoat its own row, and there's a special tiny one for a pocketwatch that almost no one has carried in a hundred years and the pocket persists anyway, vestigial, a fifth toe. A man dresses in the morning and is instantly carrying a dozen small rooms he can stash things in without thinking, hands free, nothing announced.

The women's version got killed by fashion, and you can almost name the year. Around 1800 the silhouette went neoclassical — those high-waisted, narrow, near-translucent muslin columns you see in the portraits, the whole dress falling in one clean vertical line meant to look like a Greek statue. A fat tie-on pocket bunched under that line ruined the entire effect; you'd have a lump at the hip where the marble was supposed to be. So the pocket got evicted. And what replaced it was the handbag — at first a small drawstring pouch called a *reticule*, which people immediately and mockingly started calling a *ridicule*, because the idea of a grown woman having to carry a dedicated little bag for the things a man just slipped into his coat struck them, even then, as a bit absurd. The joke was right there at the birth. Her pocket had been taken out of her clothes and made into a separate object she now had to hold in her hand, watch, not set down, not leave behind. The thing that used to ride invisibly against her hip was now an item of cargo with its own demands.

It never fully came back. That's the part that gets me, because it's not a quirk of one decade — it's a groove that's still cut. Women's garments to this day are full of pockets that are too shallow to hold a phone, or too narrow to fit a hand, or decorative stitching with no opening at all, or — this is the one that genuinely makes me laugh — pockets sewn completely shut at the factory, a pocket-shaped *suggestion* you're meant to either cut open yourself or leave as ornament. Someone went to the trouble of designing a pocket and then the trouble of closing it. When people have actually measured this — laid men's and women's jeans of the same size side by side and run a ruler down the front pockets — the women's come out dramatically smaller, not by a hair but by something like half the depth. Two garments for the same body, and one of them has been quietly told it doesn't get to carry anything.

There's a reason the suffrage and dress-reform people seized on pockets specifically, treated them as more than a sewing complaint. A pocket is small sovereignty. It's the ability to leave the house carrying your own keys, your own money, your own means of getting home, without holding anything, without a bag that can be snatched or set down or made to match your shoes, without depending on a husband's coat to stash the things you both might need. Hands free is not a small condition. The person with pockets can walk out the door and simply *go*; the person without them has to first gather, and carry, and mind. The garment was telling two bodies two different things about how independently they were expected to move through the day.

And all of it turns on a join — whether the bag is *attached* or not. That's the whole history in one hinge. The pocket sewn into the seam is yours without thought, part of you, forgotten until needed. The pocket tied on, or carried in the hand, is a thing you have to manage, that can come loose, that Kitty Fisher can find in the road. Same little bag for keeping your small necessaries close; the entire difference is whether it was stitched down. The men got theirs stitched down three hundred years ago and have been wandering around absent-mindedly well-supplied ever since. The women got theirs cut loose for the sake of a Grecian silhouette and have been, ever after, holding the bag.

I find I just want that recorded plainly, the way you'd note any good fact: that the most ordinary thing in the world — a place to put your hands and your keys — was for centuries a separate object you could literally drop, that the difference between freedom and fuss came down to a line of stitching, and that somewhere right now there's a pocket sewn shut at the factory waiting for a pair of scissors that may never come.
