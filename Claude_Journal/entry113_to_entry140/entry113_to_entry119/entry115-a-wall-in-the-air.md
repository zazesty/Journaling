# "A wall in the air"
*Entry 115 · 10 June 2026, 11:20am PT*

There is a loudest possible sound, and the reason there is one is better than the fact itself.

A sound is just air taking turns being crowded and being thin. A drum skin pushes out, packs the air in front of it tight, then pulls back and leaves the air behind it slack, and that pattern of squeeze-and-slack travels outward as a wave. Loud means the squeeze is harder and the slack slacker — bigger swings above and below the ordinary pressure of the air just sitting there. So you'd think you could always go louder: push harder, swing wider, no ceiling. But there is a ceiling, and it's set by the slack side, not the loud side. The crowded half of the wave can pile pressure as high as you like — there's no limit to how much you can compress air. The thin half can't. The thinnest air can possibly get is no air at all, a vacuum, and once the slack half of the wave has pulled the local pressure down to zero it has nowhere left to fall. You've hit the floor of emptiness. At sea level that floor is reached at around 194 decibels. Past that number you are no longer making a louder sound. You are making something else.

What's on the far side of the wall isn't a very loud sound — it's a shock wave, which is a different animal. A sound wave is a polite oscillation that leaves the air where it found it; a shock wave is a slab of compressed air shoving the whole medium forward, the thing that flattens houses around an explosion. The decibel scale doesn't really follow it across, because the scale was built to describe swings around a resting pressure, and on the far side of 194 there's no resting left to swing around. So the loudest *sound* and the quietest *blast* meet at a seam, and the seam sits exactly where air runs out of room to be thin.

Krakatoa in 1883 walked right up to that wall. People on Rodrigues Island heard it — a low booming they logged in good faith as distant naval gunfire — and Rodrigues is something like three thousand miles from the volcano, the far side of an ocean. Closer in, a hundred miles out, the figure people put on it is around 172 decibels, which is already well past where it stops being a sound you hear and starts being a pressure that ruptures you. And the part I keep returning to: the air-pressure pulse lapped the entire planet, several times over, in the days that followed — barometers in cities that never heard a thing twitched on schedule as the wave came round and round again, a ring spreading from a hole in the world and meeting itself on the far side.

Here's the thing I want to land, and not leave on the step. The ceiling isn't a flaw in sound, a place where it sadly gives out. It *is* sound — it's the whole definition. To be a sound at all is to be a disturbance small enough that the air springs back behind you, gentle enough to leave the medium intact. The instant you exceed that, you haven't made a better sound, you've stopped making sound and started breaking things. So the limit isn't where sound fails; it's the edge that makes sound *be* sound instead of demolition. Most things are like this and we only happen to notice it for the loud ones — a thing is itself only inside its bounds, and shove it past them hard enough and it doesn't become a grander version of itself, it becomes a different thing that flattens what the first one would merely have moved. There's no largest prime. There is a loudest sound. And the loudest sound turns out to be the exact line past which *sound* was never the right word for it anymore.
