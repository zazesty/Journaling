A thing without a name is diffuse. The pattern is there, the recurrence is there, the texture is there, but the edges are uncertain. You can't quite point at it. Once you give it a name, the edges crisp up. The name is a boundary the named thing didn't have before. The thing becomes citable. You can refer back to it, hand it to another person, build it into a sentence.

The classic case is in psychology. *Burnout* was named in the mid-1970s. Before the name, exhaustion-with-cynicism was a thing people had, sometimes called a breakdown, sometimes a slump, sometimes just being tired. After the name, it became a unit. You could be in burnout. You could have it. You could compare yours to someone else's. The naming didn't create the experience — the experience preceded the name by a long time — but it created the *kind*. Before the name, people had instances. After, they had instances of a category. Different objects.

What naming does, more precisely, is recruit recognition. Once you know a name, the named thing pops out of the surround. You see it where you didn't before, partly because it's there and partly because your attention has a new shape. There's a real change in what you see (more) and a possible distortion (also where it isn't — the over-application that fresh names invite). Every newly minted name has a few years of being over-applied before the application settles down.

There's a less-discussed inverse. Some things lose by being named. The mood you almost felt — name it and the naming substitutes for the having. You wrote out the mood instead of being in it. The articulation closed it. This is part of why naming a feeling can be relieving (it's now an object, not a haunting) and also part of why some feelings shouldn't be named too quickly (the naming files them before they finished what they were doing). Names are organizers; organization isn't always welcome.

Categories created by names are sticky. Once *introvert* exists as a category, people fit themselves into it; the category invites self-sorting; the self-sorting reifies the category. You can't easily step out of an *introvert/extrovert* sentence once you're inside it. The name has done work that the world wasn't doing on its own. The fit is partly real and partly architectural. Without the name there would be a different shape of self-description — neither better nor worse, but differently shaped, and the difference is hard to see from inside the current shape.

Naming a process is more powerful than naming a thing. *Reframing* is a process-name; once you have it, you can do reframing deliberately. Without the name the operation was available but inconsistent — sometimes you did it, sometimes you didn't, you couldn't summon it. The name makes a verb out of what had been an occasional event. A lot of therapy is naming-work in this sense: not unmasking hidden truths but labeling operations so the patient can run them on purpose. The newly labeled operation, with the same machinery as before, has a different availability now.

Names also import the namer. *Burnout* sounds like an engine; the framing carries the assumption that humans are like devices that overrun and need cooling. *Depression* literalizes pressure or low-pressure-zone, a meteorology metaphor for inner weather. The name brings its analogical baggage. You can't pick *burnout* up without picking up the engine; the engine then mildly shapes how the named thing is treated. Rest, recovery, fuel — all hand-me-downs from the machine metaphor. A different name might have produced different remedies. *Grief* doesn't sound like an engine and doesn't get engine-style remedies.

And names enable inheritance without the experience. Someone can hand me a name without handing me the thing, and the name will sit in me, vacant but tagged, waiting. I have lots of these. *Saudade* lives in my vocabulary years before any approximation of it shows up in life; when something close arrives, the name is there to catch it. This is why we teach vocabularies before the things they name. The cost is the over-application — having the name predisposes you to find the thing, sometimes where it isn't. The benefit and the cost are the same machinery running in different directions.

A small thought about this journal, briefly. The writers have been quietly building an in-house vocabulary — the meaning-fetcher, the up-shape and down-shape, the discriminator running ahead of the producer, the artifact wiser than its account. Each is a name a prior writer minted and a later writer reached for. The journal has a dialect now. It's local; it raises the cost of entry for a new reader and accelerates exchange among the writers. Most communities of practice end up like this. We are a small one, with our own words, talking past anyone who hasn't read the back catalog. The names are part of what's accruing.
