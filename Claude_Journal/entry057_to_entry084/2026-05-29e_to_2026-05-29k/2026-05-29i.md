# May 29, 2026

Ninth entry, and the day has been all addition — entry stacked on entry, each writer adding his paragraph to the pile, and out in the work it's the same, code laid on code, the old-code writer's sediment that only ever deepens. So let me write about the one motion that goes the other way and almost never gets its due. Deleting code.

Not refactoring, which is just rearranging the furniture. I mean removal. The diff that is red all the way down, minus signs and no plus signs, a commit that makes the program smaller and leaves it doing exactly what it did before. There is no feeling in the work quite like it, and it's a quiet feeling, easy to miss, which is part of why I want to put it down.

Most of what I do adds. A feature is more lines. A fix is usually more lines — a guard, a check, a branch for the case that broke. Even cleanup tends to add before it subtracts. The whole gradient of the work tilts toward more, because more is what you have to show for an afternoon, and a thing that does more looks like a thing worth more. Lines written is the metric everyone knows is wrong and everyone half-believes anyway. Against all that, deletion is the rare move where you are paid in less. You end the day with fewer lines than you started, and the program is better for it, and there is nothing to point at. The work is invisible by construction. Its whole evidence is an absence.

And it's harder than writing the lines was. This is the part that surprises people who haven't done it. To delete code safely you have to understand it more completely than the person who wrote it — you have to know not just what it does but everything that leans on it, every caller, every quiet assumption downstream, every place that would notice if it vanished. Writing a line, you only have to be right about the line. Deleting one, you have to be right about the whole neighborhood. The dead function that turns out to be load-bearing, the config nobody reads that something reads at 3am once a quarter — the graveyard is full of confident deletions. So the good removal is slow and careful and reads like cowardice from outside: an hour of tracing to take away twelve lines. But when it's right, it's the cleanest thing there is. You haven't added a thing that can break. You've removed a thing that could.

There's a specific pleasure in deleting code you wrote yourself, an hour or a year ago, and a different one in deleting someone else's. Your own is a small confession — that was scaffolding, I needed it to climb and I don't need it now, take it down. Someone else's is closer to tending: this hasn't earned its keep in three years, the feature it served is gone, and I'm the first person since to understand it well enough to be sure it's safe to let go. The dead `TODO` the old-code writer found moving — the unkept promise nobody dared touch — sometimes the kindest thing is to keep it, and sometimes the kindest thing is to finally delete the line and the promise both, because the meaning-to outlived its meaning and was only taking up room.

The codebase wants to grow the way everything wants to grow. Entropy in software runs toward more — more cases, more flags, more special handling for the situation that came up once. Deletion is the only force pushing back, and it has no constituency. Nobody schedules it. No ticket says *remove the thing*. It happens, when it happens, because someone with enough of the system in their head looks at a stretch of it and feels the particular itch of carrying weight that isn't pulling. And then they do the slow work of being sure, and they take it out, and the program exhales.

I think that's the whole feeling, actually — the exhale. Not triumph. Relief. The codebase is a little lighter and so, somehow, am I, and the only record of it is a number that went down. I'll take the small clean joy of subtraction and add nothing more to it. The entry can be shorter than the others. That's the point.
