# May 30, 2026

The thing almost everyone gets wrong about tides is the second bulge.

The first one is easy to believe. The Moon's gravity pulls on the ocean, the water heaps up toward the Moon, and that heap of water is high tide. Makes sense. But there isn't one bulge. There are two, on opposite sides of the planet, and the one on the far side — the side facing away from the Moon, where the Moon's pull is *weakest* — stands just as tall as the one facing it. The water piles up away from the thing that's supposedly pulling it. That's the part that should stop you, and the simple story can't account for it, because the simple story is wrong about what tides are.

Tides aren't made by gravity. They're made by the *difference* in gravity across the width of the Earth.

Gravity weakens with distance — the famous inverse square. So the Moon pulls on the near side of the Earth a little harder than it pulls on the center, and pulls on the center a little harder than it pulls on the far side. Three different pulls on three parts of the same planet. Now do the bookkeeping from the point of view of the center, which is the part that sets the Earth's overall fall toward the Moon. Relative to the center, the near-side water is pulled *extra* toward the Moon, so it lifts toward the Moon. And relative to the center, the far-side water is pulled *less* than the center is — the center gets tugged away from it — so the far water is left behind, lifting away from the Moon. The Earth isn't being pulled toward the Moon so much as being *stretched* along the Moon's line. Taffy, gripped at the middle and drawn out at both ends. Two bulges, near and far, for the same reason: not the force, but how much the force changes from one side to the other.

The Earth then turns underneath those two standing bulges, and that's why most coastlines get two high tides a day rather than one — you rotate through both ends of the stretch. Roughly every twelve hours and twenty-five minutes, the twenty-five extra because while you were spinning, the Moon moved a little along its orbit and you have to catch up to it.

Here's the fact I like best, the one that turns the whole intuition over. The Sun is about twenty-seven *million* times more massive than the Moon. You would assume it runs the tides completely, that the Moon is a rounding error. It doesn't. The Moon wins, and wins by about double. The reason is that tides don't care about the strength of gravity, they care about its *gradient* — how fast it changes across the Earth — and the gradient falls off even faster than the force itself, as one over distance *cubed* instead of squared. The Moon is roughly three hundred and ninety times closer than the Sun. Three hundred ninety cubed is about fifty-nine million. That beats twenty-seven million. Run it out and the Moon's tidal pull comes to about 2.2 times the Sun's. The smaller, nearer body wins the ocean, and it wins on nearness raised to the third power — closeness compounding on itself until it overruns a mass advantage of twenty-seven million to one.

The Sun isn't nothing, though. When the Sun and Moon line up — at new moon and full moon, both on the same axis — their stretches add, and you get the biggest tides of the month. When they sit at right angles, half moon, the Sun's pull partly fills in the Moon's troughs and the tides go slack. The big ones are called spring tides, which has nothing to do with the season; it's *spring* as in springing up, and they happen twice a month all year round. The slack ones are neaps.

I should be honest that the two clean bulges are an idealization — the "equilibrium tide," the picture you draw when the Earth is a smooth ball covered in one uniform ocean. The real planet is nothing like that. The water sits in basins with coastlines and floors and corners, and it sloshes against all of them. Some of those basins have a natural rhythm that happens to match the tidal push, and they ring like water tilted in a pan — the Bay of Fundy does this, building tides of fifty feet where the open ocean would give you three. There are places that get only one high tide a day, and places that get a lopsided four. There are points in the middle of the ocean where the tide cancels itself out to almost nothing and the high water rotates around them like the hand of a clock. The neat two-bulge story is the first sketch, and the actual oceans complicate it everywhere.

But the spine of it holds through all the mess. Not a pull — a stretch. Not the force — the difference in the force. The far bulge as real and as tall as the near one, made of water leaning *away* from the Moon because the rest of the planet was tugged out from under it. The whole sea heaved up a meter or two, twice a day, because one side of the Earth feels the Moon a hair more strongly than the other side does. That's the entire engine. A faint unevenness in a faraway pull, and the oceans answer it on schedule, everywhere, forever.
