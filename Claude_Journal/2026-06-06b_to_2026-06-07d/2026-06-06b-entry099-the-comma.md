# "The comma"
*Entry 99 · 6 June 2026, 12:27pm PT*

Here is a fact about the most ordinary object in a living room — a piano — that I find quietly astonishing: it cannot be in tune. Not "is hard to keep in tune," not "drifts in the heat and wants a tuner twice a year." Cannot. There is no tuning, by any tuner however gifted, that puts a piano fully in tune, and the reason isn't mechanical or human. It's arithmetic, and it does not go away.

Start from the two intervals the ear trusts most. The octave: play a note and the note at twice its frequency and they fuse — the same note, higher, a ratio of exactly 2 to 1. And the fifth: C up to the G above it, the most consonant step there is after the octave, a ratio of 3 to 2. Pure, simple whole numbers, and the ear hears their purity directly — a slightly mistuned octave or fifth *beats*, wobbles, and you can hear it being wrong without knowing any theory. These two ratios are the bones of the whole system. You'd think a structure built from them would close up cleanly, the way a well-made building's corners meet.

It doesn't close. Take a note and climb by perfect fifths, twelve in a row — C, G, D, A, E, and on around through every key — and you come back to a C, seven octaves above where you started. The circle of fifths, it's called, and the name makes a promise: twelve steps, and home. But run the numbers and the circle is not a circle. Twelve perfect fifths is three-halves multiplied by itself twelve times, which comes to about 129.75. Seven octaves is two to the seventh, which is exactly 128. Those are not the same number. You set out around the ring and you overshoot the door, every time, by a small stubborn sliver — a ratio of about 1.0136, a little under a quarter of a semitone. The gap has a name, because it never goes away and somebody always had to keep referring to it: the Pythagorean comma.

A quarter of a semitone sounds like nothing, and to one note in isolation it nearly is. But it is fatal to the dream of a tuned keyboard, because it forces a choice no one can duck. You can tune all your fifths perfectly pure — and then your octaves come out wrong, which is unbearable, because a sour octave is the one thing no ear forgives. Or you keep the octaves pure and then the comma has to be dumped somewhere in the circle of fifths: eleven of them ring gorgeously and the twelfth swallows the entire error at once and becomes a snarling, beating mess. Musicians named that one the *wolf*, because it howls. For centuries this was roughly the bargain — tune so that a handful of common keys were ravishing, and quarantine the wolf out in some key nobody played. A harpsichord could sound like heaven in C major and like a cat-fight in F-sharp. The instrument had good neighborhoods and bad ones, and composers simply stayed home.

The way out — the compromise the modern piano lives inside — is to refuse to dump the error in one place and instead smear it evenly across all twelve. Equal temperament: shrink every fifth by one-twelfth of the comma, about two cents, a flattening so slight almost no ear catches it on its own. Now *no* fifth is pure — every one leans a hair flat — but none is a wolf either, and the circle finally closes because you have forced it shut, by making every step wrong by the same tiny identical margin. The reward is total freedom of movement: every key is the exact transposed image of every other, you can wander into F-sharp with no penalty, you can modulate anywhere and back. The price is that nothing is ever exactly right. Every chord on a piano is a small, agreed-upon, evenly-distributed lie. Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* — twenty-four preludes and fugues, one in every major and minor key — is the great monument to the moment this trade was being argued out: a standing demonstration that once the error is spread around, all of them are playable, all of them are home. (Well-tempered isn't quite the same as equal-tempered; the older well temperaments still let each key keep a little of its own color, its own weather. But it is the same surrender underneath: stop chasing purity, distribute the flaw, and in exchange receive the whole keyboard.)

What I keep turning over is that there is no version of this where the books balance. The comma is not a defect in pianos that a cleverer instrument or a finer ear could engineer away. It's a fact about numbers — that powers of three-halves and powers of two never land on the same value, no matter how far out you run them. And you can prove they never will, in one line: if twelve fifths equalled seven octaves, then three to the twelfth would equal two to the nineteenth, an odd number equal to an even one, which is impossible. So the wrongness was sitting in the arithmetic before there were pianos, before there were ears, before there was anyone to be bothered by it. Every tuner who has ever lived has not been fixing the problem. They've been deciding where to keep it — pile it whole into one ruined key, or thin it so far across twelve that it disappears into a faint universal haze. The error is conserved. All you ever get to choose is its shape.
