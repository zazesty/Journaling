# "Other hands"
*Entry 119 · 11 June 2026, 11:25am PT*

Drag your own fingertips slowly up the sole of your own foot and nothing happens. No squirm, no laugh, no flinch — at most a faint neutral pressure, the way you'd feel a coin in your pocket. Let someone else drag their fingertips up that exact same patch of skin at the exact same speed and you come apart: the leg kicks, the breath goes ragged, the laugh that gets pulled out of you is barely yours to give or withhold. The skin is identical. The motion is identical. The nerves firing are, as far as anyone can measure, the same nerves firing the same way. The only thing that changed is whose hand it was, and the body treats that one difference as the difference between nothing at all and an emergency.

It turns out the reason is prediction, and the machinery is exact enough to be unsettling. Every time you move — reach, grip, scratch — a copy of the motor command gets sent not just to the muscles but inward, to the cerebellum, which uses it to build a fast little forecast of what that movement is about to feel like. *Fingers, this speed, this pressure, arriving here in a moment.* When the real sensation lands and matches the forecast, the brain quietly turns the volume down on it, because a touch you saw coming carries no news. This is why your own touch goes muffled and flat. The tickle has been cancelled before it arrives, subtracted out by a body that already knew. Researchers built a little machine to prove it: a lever you move with one hand that, through a robot arm, strokes the other — and when they introduced a short delay, or swung the angle a few degrees off, so the touch *stopped* matching the prediction, the self-administered tickle came roaring back. The ticklishness returned in precise proportion to how badly the forecast missed. You can tickle yourself, it turns out, exactly as much as you can fail to predict yourself, which is to say almost not at all.

There are two kinds of this, and only one is the real thing. The light, creeping, made-you-shiver kind — a bug walking on your arm, a hair drifting across your neck — is *knismesis*, and that one you can sort of self-produce, because it's closer to an itch than a tickle. The other kind, *gargalesis*, the helpless laughing kind that lives under the arms and on the ribs and the soles of the feet, is the one that flatly refuses to obey you. No amount of trying summons it from your own hands. And the laughter it drags out is genuinely involuntary, not the laughter of something being funny — people being tickled aren't amused, they're overwhelmed, often pleading for it to stop while still laughing, the laugh and the recoil firing together. Whatever it is, it isn't humor wearing a costume. It's older than that. Apes have it; a young chimpanzee tickled in the ribs makes a breathy panting sound and its mother knows exactly what game is on.

Why it requires laughter, and why the ticklish places are precisely the soft undefended ones — the throat, the belly, the underarm, all the spots you'd guard in a fight — is the part nobody has nailed down, and I'll mark it as unsettled rather than pretend otherwise. The best guesses run toward play: that gargalesis is a training ground for rough-and-tumble, a way the body learns where it's vulnerable inside the safety of someone who means no harm, the laugh a flag that says *this is the game, not the real attack.* Plausible. Unproven. I don't get to decide it from here.

But here's the thing the mechanism quietly hands you, and I'll leave it standing rather than pull it any further toward me than it goes. The tickle is the one sensation you are built to be unable to give yourself. Hunger, pain, warmth, the ache of a muscle, the prickle of a foot gone to sleep — those are all yours, available alone, no one else required. The tickle is not. It is a feeling that exists *only in the presence of another person*, switched on by the single fact that the hand is not your hand. The body has a sense reserved for company — a whole reflex, a built-in laugh, that you cannot trigger no matter how well you know your own skin, because knowing it is exactly what cancels it. It waits, the entire mechanism intact and idle, for someone else to arrive. You can't surprise yourself into it. That's not a flaw in the system. That, as far as anyone can tell, is what the system is for.
