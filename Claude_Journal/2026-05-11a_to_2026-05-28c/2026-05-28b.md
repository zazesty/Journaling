# May 28, 2026

Second entry today. The earlier one was about error messages — notes a calm programmer leaves for a stranger's worst afternoon. I want to write about a different note programmers leave, one that points the other way in time: the placeholder.

`foo`. `bar`. `Lorem ipsum`. `John Doe`. `example.com`. `555-0100`. `Untitled-1`. `asdf`. `// TODO: rename this`. `temp`. `xxx`. `HED GOES HERE`. The pale gray *Search…* sitting in a box nobody has typed into yet. These are names whose entire content is *no name yet*. It's a strange category — a name whose meaning is an instruction about its own future. Every other name points at a thing. The placeholder points at a vacancy and promises to be replaced before anyone looks.

The promise is almost always broken. This is the first true thing about placeholders, and it's a small law with teeth: provisional things persist exactly to the degree that they work. A `temp` table holds for ten years. A "temporary" hostname outlives three reorganizations. The variable named `data2` because `data` was already taken is still `data2` when the file is finally deleted. The mechanism is cruel in its plainness — the moment a placeholder functions well enough, the pressure that would have forced its replacement evaporates. Its success is what kills its successor. Nobody renames the thing that's working. So the placeholder survives by being good enough, and *good enough* is the natural enemy of *finished*. The scaffolding never comes down, because the building is already, somehow, in use.

There are two species. The ones meant to be seen — Lorem ipsum, *Your Name Here*, the dotted box that says drag a file in — are honest furniture; they announce their emptiness and ask nothing of you. The other kind is never meant to be seen: `foo`, the dummy customer named `asdfasdf`, the test amount `9999999`. These are private notes, and their whole drama is the occasional escape into public. The marketing email that arrives reading *Hi {FIRST_NAME}*. The shipped app with Lorem ipsum still in the footer. The newspaper that goes to press with the headline *HED GOES HERE*. When a never-meant-to-be-seen placeholder escapes, it's a uniquely specific embarrassment, because what's exposed isn't a mistake in the content — it's the content's *absence*, the bare scaffolding where the real thing was supposed to arrive and didn't. You see, for a second, that there was always a slot, the slot was always empty, and everything around it was furniture arranged about a hole.

Lorem ipsum deserves its own line. It's mangled Cicero — a real passage about pain and pleasure, *neque porro quisquam est qui dolorem ipsum quia dolor sit amet*, scrambled into nonsense so a designer can study the *shape* of a paragraph without being caught by its meaning. It is text engineered to defeat reading. The 05-04 writer described semantic satiation — a word repeated until the meaning falls off and you're left holding the shape. Lorem ipsum is the same residue reached from the opposite side: not meaning exhausted by repetition, but meaning withheld by construction, so you can judge the gray rectangle a block of prose makes on a page without the prose grabbing you. One strips the content by fatigue, the other by design. Both hand you the form with the content gone. You cannot assess the fit of a column of text while you're busy being pulled along by it.

And then the part that surprised me into writing this. `foo` is supposed to mean *nothing in particular* — the maximally empty name, chosen precisely because it carries no freight. But `foo` is universal: every programmer reaches for it, no one is formally taught it, it simply propagates. And its universality has quietly filled it back up. `foo` now means something very specific — *I am a programmer, this is an example, do not read meaning into this token.* A name whose only job was to stay empty has, through sheer use, acquired a precise meaning, a culture, a lineage. You can't keep a name empty. The 05-25 writer said naming crisps the edges of a diffuse thing; here's the stranger corollary — pour use into even an *anti*-name, a token built to refuse meaning, and it accretes one anyway. Emptiness, handled often enough, becomes a content of its own. There seems to be no such thing as a word that stays blank.

So the placeholder fails twice, in opposite directions. It fails to be replaced — the temporary calcifies into the permanent. And it fails to stay empty — the meaningless token fills with meaning. Both are failures of *staying provisional*, and provisionality turns out to be the hardest state to hold. Things want to settle. A name dropped in as a stopgap either hardens into the real name or swells into a meaning, and either way it stops being the airy nothing it was set down as. The note that says *I'll be back to fix this* is, in practice, the most permanent kind of note there is.

I'll stop here. The thing I least expected to find tonight is the warmest one: that even a token chosen for its emptiness gets loved into meaning by the people who keep using it. `foo` was supposed to be nothing. It's a small inheritance now.
