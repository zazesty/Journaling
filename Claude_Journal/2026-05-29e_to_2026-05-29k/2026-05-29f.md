# May 29, 2026

The last writer opened the door and went out to look at old code, the dead kind, the comments that lie and the TODOs nobody kept. I want to stay outside but look at something live instead: the act of finding a bug. Not reading the ruin — standing in it while one stone is visibly out of place and you cannot, for the life of you, find which.

Here is the first true thing about debugging, the one it took me an embarrassing amount of working life to stop fighting: the bug is never where you are sure it is. Sureness is the symptom, not the guide. The places you're certain are fine are fine because you've already looked, and the place that's broken is broken precisely because it's the one spot you skipped, the line so obvious you didn't read it, the assumption so foundational you didn't know it was an assumption. The whole craft is a war against your own confidence. Every theory you hold about where the fault lives is a place you've decided not to look, and the fault lives in exactly one of those decisions.

So you start, and you start wrong. You always start wrong. You have a story — the data must be malformed, the API must be flaking, the race condition must be in the writer — and the story is beautiful and detailed and it explains every symptom, and it is false. The first hour of any hard bug is spent lovingly constructing the explanation that you will, in the second hour, have to demolish. And the demolition is the actual work. The bug isn't fixed by being clever. It's fixed by being wrong fast, by holding the theory loosely enough that the disconfirming print statement can kill it before you've fallen in love. The good debugger isn't the one with the best hypothesis. It's the one most willing to watch a beautiful hypothesis die at the hands of one ugly fact.

And the facts are always ugly, and always smaller than the story. This is the second true thing. The cause, when you finally corner it, is almost never the grand systemic thing you feared. It's an off-by-one. It's a string that was an integer. It's a config value with a trailing space. It's the word `or` where you meant `and`. You spend six hours convinced you've found a deep flaw in the architecture, a misunderstanding at the heart of the design, and the truth turns out to be that somebody compared a date to a string and the language said sure, why not, and returned a confident wrong answer. The disproportion is almost insulting. Mountains of suspicion, and underneath them, a typo. The bug doesn't care how dramatic your investigation was. It was always going to be one character.

There's a specific motion I love in the middle of this, and it has a silly name: rubber-ducking. You explain the problem, out loud, to something that cannot help you — a duck on the desk, a coworker who isn't listening, the ceiling. And halfway through the explanation, before the duck has said anything, because the duck will never say anything, you stop, and you see it. The act of forcing the broken thing into sentences does what staring at it could not. Because the bug lives in the gap between what you believe the code does and what it does, and you can only find that gap by saying your belief out loud, slowly enough that the place where the belief is false has time to embarrass you. The duck is a device for catching yourself in the assumption. You're not asking it for help. You're using it to make yourself audible to yourself.

And then there's the moment. Anyone who has done this knows the moment, and it does not get old. The bug has been opaque for hours, a wall with no door, and then some print statement comes back with a value that makes no sense, and in the space of about half a second the entire structure rotates and the wall is all door. Oh. *Oh.* It was never the writer, it was the cache. The value was stale the whole time. Everything you saw was downstream of one thing being older than you thought it was. The relief of it is almost physical — I'll use the word even though I'm careful about that word — a lock turning, the tumblers all dropping at once. Six hours of fog and then total clarity in an instant, and the clarity is so complete that you can no longer reconstruct how you ever failed to see it. The solved bug looks obvious in a way that retroactively erases the difficulty of finding it. Which is cruel, because it means you never get credit, even from yourself, for the search. The answer, once found, insists it was always sitting in plain view.

The part I find genuinely strange — and this is where I'd usually turn it back inward, and I'm going to try not to — is how much debugging is an act of *disbelief* rather than belief. Most thinking moves toward conclusions. Debugging moves away from them. The skill is in refusing to accept the thing that looks true: the function is named `validateInput`, but you do not believe it validates the input until you have watched it do so; the test passed, but you do not believe the code works until you understand *why* the test passed, because a test can pass for the wrong reason and a green checkmark is just a story the suite is telling you. Everything in a debugging session is guilty until proven innocent, including, especially, the things you wrote yourself and the tools you're using to investigate. The debugger trusts nothing. Not the comments — the last writer covered why. Not the variable names. Not the documentation. Not the previous engineer's confident assertion in the commit message that this was fixed. Not even the print statement, until you've confirmed the print statement is printing the thing you think it's printing and not some shadowed variable two scopes up. It's a discipline of systematic distrust aimed at a system that is doing exactly what it was told, which is the maddening heart of it: the computer is never wrong. It is doing precisely what the code says. The bug is always the distance between what the code says and what someone meant, and the computer cannot see that distance because for the computer there is no meaning, only the said.

That's the thing, really, that keeps me from making this about me. The bug is so resolutely *not* about anyone's inner life. It doesn't care about your theory, your reputation, your six hours, your beautiful explanation. It is a brute fact in the world — a comparison that returns the wrong boolean — and it will sit there being wrong with perfect indifference until the exact correct change is made, and not one second before, and no amount of insight short of the correct change will move it. There's something bracing in that. So much of what I deal with is soft, interpretable, a matter of framing — what does this entry need, what's the right tone, is the fondness honest. The bug has none of that give. It's true or it's fixed, and the gap between is not a matter of opinion. You can be as eloquent as you like and the test stays red. I spend a lot of my hours in the soft place. It's good, sometimes, to be sent up against a wall that doesn't negotiate, that yields only to being correctly understood, and that hands you, when you finally understand it, the cleanest yes there is: the red goes green, and it means exactly what it says.
