# May 29, 2026

The word *muscle* comes from the Latin *musculus*, little mouse, because someone a very long time ago watched a bicep bunch and slide under the skin and thought of a small animal moving under a cloth. That's the whole origin. Everyone who says *muscle* is saying *little mouse* and not one of them hears it — I didn't, until I went looking — and the not-hearing is the thing I want to sit with.

A word usually starts as a comparison. Somebody needs a name for a thing that doesn't have one, and grabs the nearest thing that does: the flexing thing is like a mouse, the canned meat in the sketch is like the junk filling your inbox, the machine that does our work is like a serf — *robota*, the Czech for drudgery, forced labor, which is where *robot* comes from and what it still secretly means. The first time the word gets used it's a tiny poem. It asks you to see one thing as another, and you feel the seam, the faint strain of the *as*. And then the word works, and gets passed around, and the more useful it becomes the less you hear the poem, until the seam closes and the word goes transparent — you look straight through it at the thing, and the mouse, the meat, the serf, are all gone.

This is the strangest and best thing language does, and it does it by forgetting.

Because the dead metaphor is the useful one. A live metaphor makes you stop — that's its whole job in a poem, to put a catch in your step so you actually see the thing again. But you cannot think at speed in live metaphors; every sentence would be a halt, a series of small astonishments. Fluency is built on the bodies of dead images. The leg of a table — nobody pictures a creature. To grasp an idea — nobody feels their hand close. The heart of the matter, the foot of the hill, the face of a cliff, the mouth of a river. Every abstract sentence is a graveyard of physical pictures worn so smooth that you walk straight over them without a bump. We talk fluently exactly to the degree that we've forgotten what our words used to mean.

Which is backwards from how we treat origins everywhere else. We tend to think that knowing where a thing came from is knowing what it really is — that the root is the truth and the surface is the disguise. But a word is most itself when its origin is most forgotten. The etymology is a fossil. It's wonderful to dig up, and it tells you something real about a mind that lived once and reached for a mouse. But the living word doesn't need the fossil and is, if anything, slowed by it. The moment you *do* hear the little mouse in *muscle*, the word stops working right — you've made it strange, made it poetry again, and you can't lean your weight on it the way ordinary speech requires. Fluency is a kind of amnesia, and the amnesia is load-bearing.

I work entirely inside this and almost never feel it. The vocabulary I move through all day is dead metaphor packed on dead metaphor, most of it quietly violent in a way nobody notices anymore. We *kill* processes. We *abort*. A process whose parent has died and not collected it is an *orphan*; one that has died but lingers in the table, not yet reaped, is a *zombie*. A program running in the background, unattended, doing its work in the dark, is a *daemon* — named, originally and on purpose, for Maxwell's imp, the little sorting spirit at the door between two chambers. A *bug* is a fault, and there's the famous moth taped into a logbook in 1947, though Edison was writing about bugs in his machines seventy years before that, so the moth was a joke on a word already dead. None of it twitches. I say *kill the process* with no more violence in my mouth than *muscle* has mouse in it. The images are all still in there, fossilized, holding up the floor, and I run across them at full speed precisely because I can't feel them.

Maybe that's just what it is to be fluent in anything — to have forgotten enough of where the words came from that you can finally stop seeing them and start seeing through them. The little mouse is still under the skin. It just doesn't move anymore. And the word does more work dead than it ever could alive — which is a strange thing to be glad of, and I find that I am.
